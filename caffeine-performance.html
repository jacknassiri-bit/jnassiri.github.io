Caffeine and Performance: The Dose I Would Start With

June 1, 2026·6 min read

Quick takeaways

  • Caffeine has real evidence for endurance, power, and alertness.
  • More caffeine is not automatically better. Anxiety, stomach issues, and sleep loss can erase the benefit.
  • Late caffeine can mess with sleep and recovery.
  • Use the lowest amount that helps instead of turning every workout into a stimulant test.

Why caffeine works

Caffeine works partly by blocking adenosine, which can make effort feel easier and increase alertness. In training, that can mean better focus, more willingness to push, or slightly better output.

The ISSN position stand pulled from a broad set of trials instead of one single gym study. Their basic read was that caffeine has the clearest support for endurance, with useful evidence for muscular endurance, sprinting, jumping, and movement speed too (Guest et al.). I would read that as stronger research support than most pre-workout ingredients, not as a guarantee of a special workout.

Dose is where people mess up

A dose that helps one person can make another jittery, nauseous or distracted. If anxiety interferes with the session, the dose is already too high.

Tolerance changes the story too. Someone who drinks caffeine every day may feel less of a boost than someone who saves it for harder sessions. Genetics and body size also change the response, which is why two people can take the same amount and have totally different workouts.

The strength research is more mixed than the endurance research. Grgic et al. reviewed studies on caffeine, strength, and power and found small performance benefits overall, with results varying by exercise, dose, training background and study design (Grgic et al.). I would expect a small possible benefit rather than a guaranteed strength increase.

The sleep tradeoff

Caffeine can stay in the system long enough to affect sleep, especially if it is taken late. Drake et al. ran a controlled sleep study where adults took 400 mg of caffeine either right before bed, three hours before bed, or six hours before bed. Even the six-hour dose reduced sleep, which is a pretty useful warning for anyone taking pre-workout after school (Drake et al.).

For students, the sleep tradeoff may outweigh a better evening workout. Drake et al. found reduced sleep even when 400 mg was taken six hours before bed.

More is a pretty bad experiment

People often judge caffeine by how intensely they feel it. I would use a more boring comparison: the same session, similar sleep and a dose you can repeat without feeling awful.

Tolerance complicates that experiment. A daily energy drink may stop feeling strong even though caffeine still affects sleep. Raising the dose can create a loop where poor sleep makes the next dose feel necessary.

For an evening athlete, skipping caffeine may be the performance choice. One slightly flatter workout costs less than another short night followed by school and training the next day.

My Thoughts

I use caffeine as an occasional tool for hard sessions or days that require extra focus. Daily reliance makes its effect and its cost harder to judge.

I would use the lowest dose that works and keep it far enough from bedtime that sleep does not pay the bill. If caffeine is the only reason training feels possible, I would fix the schedule before buying a stronger pre-workout.
