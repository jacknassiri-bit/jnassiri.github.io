Supplements: What Earned a Place in My Gym Bag

May 4, 2026·7 min read

Quick takeaways

  • Creatine, caffeine, and protein are the strongest basic performance supplements.
  • A supplement should solve a problem you can name.
  • Claims get weaker when they rely on proprietary blends, tiny studies, or influencer proof.
  • The best supplement stack is still useless if training, food, and sleep are bad.

The boring stuff usually wins

The supplements with the best evidence are familiar. Creatine helps repeated high-intensity work, caffeine can improve performance, and protein powder makes a daily protein target easier to hit. The huge supplement market leaves plenty of room for more exciting claims.

The ISSN review on exercise and sports nutrition puts its strongest support behind a small group of ingredients compared with the size of the market (Kerksick et al.). I would start with that short list.

How to spot weak marketing

A proprietary blend prevents buyers from comparing the dose with the cited research. Huge claims supported by tiny studies or before-and-after photos give me another reason to leave the product on the shelf.

No capsule can supply progressive training or enough food. I would fix those gaps before expecting a supplement to change the result.

I also check what outcome the research measured. A higher blood level or a change in soreness is less convincing than better performance across repeated trials. The result should match the promise on the front of the container.

What is worth considering

Creatine monohydrate is the easiest yes for most healthy lifters because it is cheap, studied, and useful. Caffeine can help when it does not wreck sleep. Protein powder offers the convenience of food in a scoop. Electrolytes mainly help people who sweat heavily or train in heat.

Everything else should solve a problem you can name. Otherwise I would save the money.

Finally, I ask whether food or sleep already solves the problem. Protein powder can be convenient when lunch is rushed. It has no special power over a normal meal. A good supplement makes a useful habit easier. It should not just make the plan look advanced.

My Thoughts

I do not think supplements are bad. I just think supplement culture makes people skip the order of operations. Build the plan first, then buy the thing if it still solves a real problem.

My filter starts with the study and the dose. Then I ask whether the product solves a problem I have. If it does not, the price is easy to judge: save the money.
