Beta-Alanine: Who Benefits From the Tingling Supplement

August 13, 2026·9 min read

Quick takeaways

  • Beta-alanine helps make carnosine, which buffers acid during hard efforts.
  • The clearest benefit is for hard exercise that lasts roughly one to four minutes, or sometimes up to ten minutes.
  • The tingling feeling is common and usually harmless, but it does not show that the supplement is working better.
  • It is a daily loading supplement, not a pre-workout you only take when you want a boost.

What beta-alanine does

Beta-alanine works slowly by raising muscle carnosine, a compound that helps buffer hydrogen ions during intense exercise. It takes repeated daily doses to change carnosine levels, so the timing of one scoop before a workout has little importance.

The International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand is a good starting point because it lays out the mechanism and the dosing in normal terms. Trexler et al. concluded that four weeks of 4 to 6 grams per day can raise muscle carnosine, that beta-alanine appears safe in healthy people at recommended doses, and that its best-supported performance window is hard exercise lasting about one to four minutes (Trexler et al.).

The one-to-four-minute window narrows the athletes who are likely to benefit. A one-rep max is usually too short for the buffering effect to show up much, while a relaxed long run is too aerobic for it to be the main limiter. Repeated sprints, rowing pieces and hard high-rep sets sit closer to the conditions studied.

The meta-analysis version is more boring than the ad version

Hobson et al. published an early meta-analysis in Amino Acids that included 15 manuscripts, 57 exercise measures, 23 exercise tests, 18 supplementation protocols, and 360 total participants. The beta-alanine groups improved exercise outcomes more than placebo, with the strongest signal in exercise capacity rather than performance. They estimated a median improvement of about 2.85 percent after a median total intake of 179 grams (Hobson et al.).

A median improvement of 2.85 percent can make a difference in a close event. It gives no reason to expect 40 pounds on a lift or a transformed mile time from the supplement alone.

The same meta-analysis found no clear benefit for exercise lasting under 60 seconds, while exercise lasting 60 to 240 seconds improved more consistently (Hobson et al.). That lines up with the mechanism. If the effort ends before acid-base stress really builds, extra buffering does not have much time to help.

The newer review found a small effect

Saunders et al. later published a larger systematic review and meta-analysis in the British Journal of Sports Medicine. It included 40 double-blind, placebo-controlled studies, 65 exercise protocols, 70 outcome measures, and 1,461 participants. The overall effect was small but statistically significant, and exercise duration was the biggest factor shaping whether beta-alanine helped (Saunders et al.).

Their subgroup analysis found the largest practical signal in efforts lasting 0.5 to 10 minutes, with bigger effects for exercise capacity than for performance. Capacity tests are often done to exhaustion, so they may be more sensitive to a buffering supplement. Real sport is messier because pacing, tactics, skill, and motivation all mix into the result (Saunders et al.).

I would describe beta-alanine as a fatigue-management supplement for a specific kind of hard work. It may fit repeated high-intensity intervals or sets limited by the burning drop-off. For mostly low-rep strength work with long rests, creatine is probably the more obvious first choice.

The tingling is not the goal

The famous side effect is paresthesia, which is the skin tingling or prickly feeling people get after taking beta-alanine. It can feel intense, especially if someone takes a big dose all at once. Trexler et al. noted that the tingling can be reduced by splitting doses or using sustained-release forms (Trexler et al.). The tingling only shows that the dose hit your system in a way your nerves noticed.

The dosing idea is simple: take it daily, usually around 4 to 6 grams total, split into smaller doses if the tingling annoys you. It takes weeks because the goal is to raise muscle carnosine over time. Taking a scoop right before one workout because the label says endurance is missing the point.

My Thoughts

Beta-alanine has a supported mechanism and a small performance effect in the right setting. The benefit is specific, and the athlete still has to train hard enough for that kind of fatigue to be the limiting problem.

If I were ranking supplements for most lifters, I would still put creatine first. Beta-alanine is more situational. It fits athletes who live in that nasty middle zone where the set, round, interval, or race lasts long enough to burn but short enough to stay intense.
