Medical AI Enters the Real World: The Results Are More Complicated Than Expected

September 7, 2026 · 12 min read

Quick takeaways

  • Medical AI is moving from retrospective tests into randomized trials with real patients.
  • Several capable systems have failed to improve the outcomes that mattered most.
  • Other studies show meaningful gains when AI is given a narrow job inside a workable clinical process.
  • The question is becoming less about whether a model is accurate and more about whether the surrounding system can use its answer.

For years, medical AI has looked strongest in carefully controlled tests. A model reads a set of scans, answers medical questions, or predicts which patients will get sick. Its score is compared with a doctor’s score, and the headline practically writes itself.

That kind of testing matters, but a hospital is much harder than a benchmark. Patients arrive with incomplete histories. Clinicians are rushed. Software has to fit into an electronic record that everyone already complains about. Even a correct warning may go nowhere if the next scan cannot be scheduled or nobody knows who is supposed to respond.

In 2026, several large clinical studies started giving us a better look at that gap. The results are useful precisely because they are messy. Some systems reduced workload or caught findings that had been overlooked. Others performed their assigned task without improving what happened to the patient. That is not the clean story medical AI companies usually sell, but it is probably the story medicine needs.

The benchmark era was never enough

A retrospective study asks what an algorithm would have predicted using data that already exist. A prospective trial asks what happens when people actually use it. A randomized trial goes further by comparing patients or clinical periods with and without the intervention. Each step removes some of the ways an impressive result can disappear in practice.

The difference is easy to underestimate. Imagine an algorithm that correctly identifies suspicious chest X-rays several hours sooner. That sounds useful. But the patient still needs a radiologist’s report, a referral, a CT appointment, a biopsy and eventually treatment. If the bottleneck is CT capacity, moving one image higher on a worklist may change almost nothing.

This is why a model’s sensitivity, specificity or area under the curve cannot answer the whole clinical question. Those numbers describe prediction. They do not tell us whether doctors trust the alert, whether patients receive faster care, whether false alarms create extra procedures, or whether anyone ultimately gets healthier.

A primary-care LLM looked safe but did not improve the main outcome

One of the most important tests came from 16 primary-care facilities in Kenya. Researchers randomized clinical officers to use an electronic medical record with or without assistance from a large language model. The trial included 9,691 patients and focused on treatment failures during the following 14 days (Agweyu et al.).

Treatment failure occurred in 2.2% of patients in the AI-assisted group and 2.0% in the control group. The difference was not statistically significant. Investigators did not find a serious safety signal related to the intervention, which matters, but the trial also did not show the hoped-for improvement.

That finding is more informative than another report showing that an LLM can pass an exam. The clinicians already had judgment, local experience and responsibility for the patient. Adding generated recommendations did not automatically make that combination better.

The trial does not prove that LLM support is useless. It tested one system, one workflow and a relatively uncommon short-term outcome. Any real benefit may have been too modest for the study to detect. Still, it sets a higher bar: if a tool is supposed to improve treatment, researchers should eventually measure treatment rather than settle for how convincing its answers sound.

Ninety-three thousand chest X-rays and no faster cancer pathway

The LungIMPACT trial tested AI prioritization on 93,326 primary-care chest X-rays in the United Kingdom. When prioritization was active, images the algorithm considered suspicious could move up the radiology worklist. The hope was that faster attention would lead to earlier CT scans and earlier lung-cancer diagnoses (Woznitza et al.).

It did not. Median time to CT was 53 days in both groups. Lung cancer was diagnosed after a median of 44 days with prioritization and 46 days without it, a difference that was not significant. Treatment did not start sooner, and cancers were not found at an earlier stage.

The algorithm did identify findings, but the pathway around it did not move faster. That distinction may be one of the most important implications of the study. Hospitals can buy an AI product more easily than they can create CT capacity, shorten referral queues or redesign who acts on a warning.

There was also substantial disagreement between the AI and radiology reports. Expert review found actionable findings in some of those discordant cases, showing that the system still contained useful information. But useful information and an improved cancer pathway were not the same result.

Mammography showed what a narrower job can accomplish

A breast-screening trial in Spain produced a more encouraging outcome. Researchers prospectively evaluated 31,301 women using standard double reading and a partially autonomous AI strategy. In the AI pathway, examinations classified as low risk were treated as normal without human reading, while the remaining cases received radiologist review with AI support (Elías-Cabot et al.).

Radiologist workload fell by 63.6%, and the cancer-detection rate rose from 6.3 to 7.3 cancers per 1,000 screenings. That is a meaningful result. The model was not being asked to manage an entire disease or repair a hospital pathway. It was given a defined role in a repetitive screening process.

The tradeoff was that recalls increased overall, particularly in digital mammography. More recalls can mean more imaging, more biopsies and more anxiety for people who do not have cancer. The results were also not identical across ordinary digital mammography and three-dimensional tomosynthesis.

So even this success needs a careful reading. Reducing workload is valuable, especially where screening programs lack enough radiologists. Finding more cancers is valuable too. But a screening system has to balance missed cancers against the costs of calling more patients back.

The liver study points toward AI as a safety net

Another promising study used an AI system called LiON to analyze contrast-enhanced CT scans for liver malignancies. After multicenter retrospective validation, researchers deployed it as an additional reader in routine practice across 10,333 patients (LiON investigators).

The collaboration found 51 previously overlooked lesions, including 15 malignancies. It led to 37 amended radiology reports, 22 escalations to multidisciplinary teams and changes in clinical management for a subset of patients.

This use feels different from asking AI to replace the first interpretation. The radiologist still reads the scan. The system then serves as a second chance to catch something important. Medicine already uses double checks in situations where a miss carries a high cost, and AI may make that kind of safety net easier to scale.

The study was single-arm, so there was no simultaneous control group showing what would have happened without LiON. Its accuracy was impressive, but the researchers correctly called for comparative trials across more health systems. It is a promising implementation study rather than final proof that the system improves survival.

Workflow is part of the medical intervention

These studies make more sense when the software and the workflow are treated as one intervention. An alert sitting passively in a chart is different from an alert routed to a named clinician who has a clear next step. A model that clears routine mammograms is different from one that predicts a future event nobody has the capacity to prevent.

Human behavior matters too. Clinicians may ignore a tool that creates too many alerts, distrust a score they cannot explain, or use the system differently when the ward gets busy. None of that appears on a clean model-performance table, but it can decide whether the product helps.

There is also a risk that efficiency becomes the only outcome administrators care about. A system that lets one radiologist cover more scans may save money while increasing mental load or making rare mistakes harder to notice. Workload reduction should be measured alongside patient outcomes, false alarms and the quality of the work clinicians still have to do.

What should count as proof?

The answer depends on the claim. If a company says its model detects cancer more accurately, a carefully designed diagnostic study may be enough to test that statement. If it says the product gets patients treated sooner, researchers need to measure treatment time. If it promises better health, the evidence eventually has to reach patient outcomes.

Not every tool requires a mortality trial. Some effects would take years or enormous sample sizes to measure. Faster documentation, fewer missed findings and lower clinician workload can all be legitimate benefits. The important part is matching the evidence to what the product is being sold to do.

We should also expect results to change by hospital. A workflow that helps a national screening program may fail in a small clinic with different equipment, staffing and patient demographics. Medical AI cannot be evaluated once and assumed to work everywhere forever.

My Thoughts

I think this is the point where medical AI becomes more interesting. The field is leaving the stage where every impressive score feels like a breakthrough. Now the technology has to survive contact with scheduling systems, limited staff, anxious patients and all the ordinary friction of health care.

The disappointing trials are part of that progress. They tell hospitals where a correct prediction is getting lost. The positive trials show that AI works best when the job is narrow, the response is clear and clinicians remain connected to the decision.

The standard I would use is simple: after the algorithm gives its answer, what changes for the patient? If nobody acts, if the next appointment is still two months away, or if the tool mostly creates another alert, the technical achievement did not finish the job. When it catches an overlooked cancer or safely removes routine work, then the result starts to feel real.
