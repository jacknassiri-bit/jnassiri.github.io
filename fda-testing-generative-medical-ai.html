Testing Medical AI: Why the FDA Is Borrowing an Idea From Physician Training

August 24, 2026 · 7 min read

Quick takeaways

  • The FDA released a discussion paper in August 2026 rather than a final regulation.
  • One proposed approach combines benchmark-style competency testing with clinical confirmation.
  • The agency is also considering risk-based monitoring after a product reaches patients.
  • Foundation models and AI agents create new questions because one underlying model may support many medical tasks.

A conventional medical device is supposed to do a defined job in a predictable way. Generative AI is harder to pin down. The same model can summarize a chart, answer a patient, suggest a diagnosis and help plan treatment. Its response changes with the prompt, the available records and sometimes a software update.

In August 2026, the FDA released a discussion paper asking how generative-AI medical devices should be evaluated. It is not a finished rule. The agency is requesting public feedback, but the framework shows how regulators are thinking about a technology that does not fit neatly into older device categories (FDA).

A competency test followed by clinical confirmation

The FDA describes a possible two-stage approach inspired at a high level by physician assessment. First, a device would face nonclinical benchmarking across relevant capabilities. Those could include medical knowledge, analysis, communication, safety behavior and performance across different populations.

Passing a benchmark would not finish the evaluation. The second stage would clinically confirm that the product performs its intended job in the setting where it will be used. That distinction matters because an AI can answer isolated questions correctly and still fail when the medical record is incomplete or the workflow is chaotic.

The paper also asks whether independent third parties should maintain hidden test datasets, conduct assessments or serve as expert adjudicators. Sequestered evaluations could make it harder for developers to tune a model specifically to a public test.

Risk depends on what the AI can do

A system that drafts discharge instructions does not carry the same immediate risk as one that recommends a medication dose. The FDA’s possible framework considers both the seriousness of the decision and how independently the AI acts.

That creates a spectrum. A low-risk tool may only need strong benchmarking and user oversight. A system influencing diagnosis or treatment could require clinical evidence and more aggressive monitoring. An agent that takes actions across several steps may deserve closer review than a model that produces one suggestion for a clinician.

Approval cannot be the last check

Generative models may drift as hospitals change their software, patient populations or data. Developers may update the underlying model while keeping the medical product’s interface nearly identical. The FDA paper therefore discusses postmarket monitoring based on risk.

That monitoring could include performance reports, incident review and checks for new failure patterns. The hard question is deciding what change is large enough to require another regulatory submission. Updating a spelling error is different from replacing the foundation model that produces every answer.

The foundation-model problem

Many medical products may be built on the same large foundation model. If that base model changes, dozens of tools could change with it. Responsibility is then spread across the foundation-model developer, the medical-device company and the hospital deploying it.

AI agents add another layer. A chatbot that explains a lab result is one thing. A system that retrieves records, chooses tools and carries out a multi-step clinical task can fail through a chain of individually reasonable actions. Regulators may need to evaluate the full behavior rather than test each component alone.

My Thoughts

I like the basic idea of making medical AI demonstrate competence and then prove itself in clinical use. It acknowledges that medical knowledge tests are useful without pretending they recreate patient care.

The harder part will be continuing the evaluation after release. A doctor has a license, an employer and a clear professional responsibility. With generative AI, accountability can disappear into a chain of companies and software updates. Any final framework should make it obvious who has to notice a problem, who reports it and who is responsible for fixing it.

The FDA has not settled those questions yet. That is what makes the discussion paper worth following. The rules being developed now may decide how much autonomy medical AI receives later.
