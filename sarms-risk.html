SARMs: Marketed Like Supplements, Acting More Like Drugs

July 6, 2026·7 min read

Quick takeaways

  • SARMs are not FDA-approved dietary supplements.
  • They act on androgen receptors, which is why they can affect muscle and hormones.
  • Risks include liver injury, HDL changes, testosterone suppression, and contaminated or mislabeled products.
  • The cleaner-than-steroids marketing is much stronger than the real safety evidence.

Why SARMs sound appealing

SARMs, or selective androgen receptor modulators, are marketed as if they are the smart version of steroids: muscle gain with fewer side effects. That pitch is powerful because it sounds scientific and safer at the same time.

“Selective” describes how these compounds interact with androgen receptors. It does not guarantee that their effects stay in muscle tissue.

The supplement label is misleading

SARMs are often sold online as supplements or research chemicals. The FDA warns that bodybuilding products containing them have been tied to heart attack, stroke, serious liver damage and other safety problems (FDA). Their polished branding does not change those risks.

Labeling is another problem. Van Wagoner et al. bought 44 products marketed as SARMs online and tested what was actually in them. Only about half contained a SARM, several had unapproved drugs, and many labels did not match the dose or ingredients in the bottle (Van Wagoner et al.). So even if someone thinks they accepted the risk, they may not know what drug or dose they are taking.

The risks show up in real cases

Leciejewska et al. analyzed suspected SARM-related adverse-event cases, including drug-induced liver injury reports (Leciejewska et al.). Case reports cannot provide an exact risk for each user. They do document serious outcomes that casual marketing usually leaves out. Testosterone suppression is also consistent with the androgen pathway.

Cycles and before-and-after photos are anecdotes from people experimenting with drugs that are unapproved for bodybuilding. They cannot establish safety, especially when the product may not match its label.

My thoughts

SARMs are a clear example of fitness marketing getting ahead of medicine. The name sounds precise, although the products and their risks are often poorly controlled.

I judge SARMs by the standards used for drugs. A supplement-looking website does not change what the compound does inside the body.

The label may be the first failure

The online-product study is the detail I would show anyone considering SARMs. Only about half of the 44 products contained a SARM, and some contained other unapproved drugs. A person cannot make an informed risk decision when the bottle itself is unreliable.

Hormone suppression also changes the timeline. The cycle may last weeks, but recovery can last longer and may not follow the plan described in a forum. Bloodwork and symptoms do not care that the product was advertised as “selective.”

The evidence may change someday if a SARM earns approval for a medical use. That would require a known dose, a manufactured product and trials that measure benefit against harm. The current online market offers none of those protections.
