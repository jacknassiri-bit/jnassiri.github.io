AI as a Second Reader: The System That Found Overlooked Liver Cancers

August 31, 2026 · 7 min read

Quick takeaways

  • LiON analyzes contrast-enhanced CT scans and clinical information for possible liver malignancy.
  • In routine practice, AI–human review identified 51 overlooked lesions, including 15 malignancies.
  • Some findings led to amended reports, specialist-team review and changes in patient management.
  • The clinical study lacked a randomized control group, so the outcome benefit remains uncertain.

A radiologist can read thousands of images in a normal workweek. Most decisions are made correctly, but subtle lesions can be missed when scans are complicated, the liver already has disease, or the original examination was ordered for another reason.

A 2026 study tested whether AI could act as another set of eyes. The Liver DiagnOsis Network, or LiON, combined multiphase CT imaging with clinical information to identify possible liver malignancies. Its most interesting result was not a score on a test set. It was what happened when the system disagreed with reports in routine care.

From validation to the clinical workflow

LiON was trained using 6,443 patients and retrospectively evaluated across more than 22,000 patients from multiple cohorts. It maintained strong diagnostic performance in patients with fatty liver disease and cirrhosis, two settings that can make liver imaging harder to interpret (LiON investigators).

The researchers then placed the system into routine practice as an additional reader for 10,333 patients. It did not replace the radiologist’s report. Instead, it created a second opportunity to flag a lesion that might deserve another look.

What the system caught

AI–human collaboration identified 51 previously overlooked lesions. Fifteen were malignant. The review led to 37 amended radiology reports and 22 cases being escalated to multidisciplinary teams. Some patients had their clinical management changed.

Those numbers are modest compared with the total number of scans, but the denominator is not the whole story for the individual patient. A cancer found because of a second check can change the timing and options for treatment.

This also points toward a role for AI that feels more believable than total automation. Medicine already uses backup checks in pharmacies, laboratories and operating rooms. An algorithm that reviews every eligible scan may provide a version of that safety net without requiring a second radiologist to reread everything.

The limitations still matter

The clinical portion was a single-arm trial. Everyone received the AI-supported workflow, so there was no randomized group experiencing normal care at the same time. We do not know how many reports might have been corrected later through ordinary follow-up, or whether earlier detection improved survival.

A safety-net system can also create false alarms. Every flag takes attention, and follow-up testing can expose patients to cost, radiation and worry. A useful second reader has to catch enough meaningful misses without overwhelming clinicians with low-value disagreements.

The study was conducted within particular hospitals and patient populations. Independent validation elsewhere will be important before assuming the same performance across scanners, reporting styles and rates of liver disease.

My Thoughts

This may be one of the clearest ways for AI to earn trust in radiology. The doctor makes the main interpretation, and the algorithm looks for a costly miss. Responsibility remains visible, while the system contributes something a tired human cannot: the ability to review every case with the same level of attention.

The next step should be a comparative trial that measures more than detection. I would want to know how often the alerts change care, how many unnecessary tests they create and whether cancers are treated earlier. Still, finding overlooked malignancies in a live workflow is closer to meaningful medical progress than another claim that a model beat a benchmark.
